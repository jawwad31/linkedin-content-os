# Content Strategy Method

The unit of value is a useful belief shift in the right buyer. Reach is not the
planning objective. A strong idea helps a specific buyer see a decision,
constraint, risk, or operating problem more accurately and gives the founder a
claim they can defend publicly.

This file controls idea readiness and the visible strategy. It does not write
the hook, body, or CTA. Those execution choices are planned separately so the
strategy remains simple enough for a human to inspect.

## The Eight-Part Strategy

Every ready idea must answer these eight questions.

### 1. Target ICP

Choose one buyer group and one relevant situation. Prefer a narrow description
such as "founders hiring their first revenue operator" over "B2B founders."

The target must:

- come from the saved ICP and selected source;
- name a role, stage, problem, or decision the buyer recognizes;
- be narrow enough that the opening can speak directly to it;
- avoid inventing a segment absent from the business context.

### 2. Current Buyer Belief

State the incomplete, risky, or misleading belief the target buyer is likely to
hold before reading. It must be plausible and directly related to the source.

Good current beliefs are:

- concrete enough to disagree with;
- about a decision or operating model, not a vague emotion;
- written as one sentence;
- free of unsupported "everyone" or "most teams" claims.

### 3. Desired Buyer Belief

State what the buyer should believe after reading. It should be more useful and
more precise than the current belief, not merely its rhetorical opposite.

The shift should change at least one of:

- what the buyer notices;
- what they prioritize;
- how they diagnose a problem;
- how they compare options;
- what they do next.

### 4. Core Argument

Write one defensible sentence that moves the reader from the current belief to
the desired belief. This is the post's thesis, not its title and not a list.

The core argument must:

- make one claim;
- connect the selected source to the buyer's situation;
- have a clear practical consequence;
- remain useful without a sales pitch;
- stay inside the allowed claims.

### 5. Primary Source Anchor

Name the exact source detail the argument depends on. The anchor is the
specific event, founder reaction, TrendCard shift, source article detail, or
approved evidence point that keeps the idea grounded.

Do not use a generic source label. "The TrendCard" is not an anchor. "Buyers are
moving vendor evaluation from feature checklists to implementation risk" is.

### 6. Proof to Use

List only evidence that can appear in the post. Separate proof from context:

- a Founder Experience may support first-person events, decisions, lessons,
  documented before/after states, and exact outcomes present in the entry;
- a Trend Reaction may support the founder's supplied interpretation while the
  linked TrendCard supplies timing and market context;
- a TrendCard may support only the cited market signal and careful synthesis;
- Approved Evidence may support exact named results, metrics, examples, or
  credentials that were explicitly saved.

Empty proof is valid when the idea is a careful qualitative argument. Missing
proof is not permission to fabricate specificity.

### 7. Angle Type

Select the reasoning lens that best expresses the core argument. Angle type
answers "how are we making the argument?" Examples include diagnosis,
comparison, myth correction, process breakdown, and operator POV.

Angle type is different from content archetype:

- angle type is the intellectual move;
- content archetype is the reader-facing kind of post;
- body framework is the order used to develop the body.

Do not choose an angle merely for novelty. Choose the angle the evidence can
support with the least stretching.

### 8. Risk Notes and Readiness

Risk notes identify what the writer must not imply, overstate, generalize, or
invent. Include source-mode constraints, missing context, ambiguous causality,
and proof limitations.

Set readiness to:

- `ready` when all eight parts are specific, compatible, and defensible;
- `blocked` when the core argument needs missing proof, the source cannot
  support the planned angle, or the idea duplicates a recent argument.

Only `ready` ideas may be stored.

## Source-Mode Strategy Rules

### Founder Experience

Use the real event as the center of gravity. The post may use first person and
lived-experience framing, but only for details actually present in the record.

Strong strategic moves:

- mistake to operating rule;
- before/after when both states are documented;
- behind-the-build process;
- case example;
- operator POV derived from the lesson.

Do not generalize one event into a universal market fact. Phrase broad lessons
as a practical inference, not as proof that all companies behave the same way.

### TrendCard

Treat the card and cited articles as market context. Do not claim the founder
personally observed, tested, or caused the shift.

Strong strategic moves:

- trend implication for the ICP;
- diagnostic consequence;
- decision criteria;
- comparison;
- FAQ answer;
- careful myth correction.

Use calibrated language when evidence is directional. Do not turn a source
summary into a guaranteed forecast.

### Trend Reaction

Use the founder's supplied reaction as the primary point of view. The linked
TrendCard explains why the reaction matters now.

Strong strategic moves:

- agreement with a sharper implication;
- contradiction with a defensible alternative;
- a gap the trend discussion misses;
- an operator observation grounded in the reaction;
- comparison or myth correction.

Do not add a stronger founder opinion than the saved reaction contains.

## Commercial Relevance Without Pitching

An idea is commercially relevant when the belief affects a real buyer outcome:

- cost or wasted effort;
- risk or delay;
- quality of a decision;
- speed of implementation;
- ability to diagnose a problem;
- confidence in choosing an approach;
- readiness to seek help.

The plan may connect to the offer by showing why the problem matters. It should
not force product claims, a discovery call, or a sales CTA into every post.

## Distinct-Idea Standard

A batch may contain three to five ideas when the source supports them. Fewer is
better than filler. Ideas are distinct only when both the core argument and
angle type differ meaningfully.

Changing only the hook, framework, or wording does not create a new idea.

Reject an idea when it:

- repeats a recent core argument;
- uses a different taxonomy label for the same reasoning;
- depends on the same source detail and reaches the same conclusion;
- broadens beyond the target ICP to manufacture variety;
- needs unsupported proof to feel substantial.

## Resource-Post Strategy

A resource post is allowed only when an active, approved Content Resource is
selected. The resource must solve the same problem as the core argument and be
useful to the target ICP.

When a resource is selected:

- use `resource_post` as the content archetype;
- use `comment_gate` as the CTA type;
- explain enough value in the post that the resource is not a bait-and-switch;
- state the exact keyword once;
- never promise automatic delivery;
- never invent a guide, template, report, tool, or result.

At most one resource/comment-gated idea may appear in a generated batch. If
recent-post gating rules make it ineligible, plan a non-gated idea instead.

## Planning Decision Sequence

Use this sequence before selecting execution choices:

1. Inspect the selected source and identify the strongest concrete anchor.
2. Match the anchor to one narrow ICP situation.
3. State the current and desired beliefs.
4. Form one core argument that can move the belief.
5. List publishable proof and explicit boundaries.
6. Select the best-supported angle type.
7. Record risk notes.
8. Mark ready only if the founder could defend every planned claim on a sales
   call using the supplied source and Approved Evidence.

Only after the idea is ready should the planner select content archetype,
post structure, body framework, hook type, CTA type, argument outline, and
optional resource.
