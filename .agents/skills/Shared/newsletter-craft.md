# Shared: Newsletter Craft

**Applies to:** Planning, Writing and QA.
**Where voice and reader come from:** the account's business profile (`profile` in the input). This file holds craft that does not depend on any one business. It sets no house voice and no preset audiences.

## 1. Reader and voice come from the profile

- **Reader:** `profile.primaryIcp`, `audienceUnderstanding`, `painPoints`, `questionsAndDoubts` describe the business reader. `personalReaders` describes who reads the founder personally. The edition request says which one the issue is for. Never invent reader research, quotes or survey findings.
- **Voice:** follow `voiceGuide`, `founderVoiceSample`, `founderVoiceExamples`, `phrasesToUse`, `phrasesToAvoid`, `principles` and `boundaries`. Voice examples calibrate style only; never copy their claims, stories or distinctive wording. When the profile has no voice material, write in a plain, direct, evidence-conscious style.
- **Narrator:** use "I" only for author-provided experience (see the app rules). Use the company voice only when `businessMentions` or the request asks for it.
- **Never contradict the profile.** `bannedTopics`, `boundaries` and `phrasesToAvoid` always win over craft advice here.

## 2. Reader questions every plan must answer

1. Who is the primary reader, and what decision, task or responsibility do they own right now?
2. What could change for them because of the selected material?
3. What does it cost to misunderstand or ignore it (without inventing a number)?
4. What do they already know, and what needs explaining?
5. What will a helpful issue let them do next?

If the material has no genuine reader consequence, propose a narrower angle or a shorter issue. Do not manufacture urgency.

## 3. Relevance ladder

1. **Direct impact:** a documented change affects something the reader already runs.
2. **Near-term decision:** the material supports a plausible upcoming choice or test.
3. **Emerging signal:** interesting but uncertain; frame it explicitly as "watch".
4. **General curiosity:** weak operational consequence; use sparingly.

A story with a direct workflow consequence deserves deeper treatment than a loud headline that cannot be tied to the reader's work.

## 4. Technical depth

- **Novice:** define terms, explain cause and effect, expand acronyms.
- **Intermediate:** focus on mechanisms, comparisons and execution choices.
- **Advanced:** include edge cases, measurement limits and counterarguments.

Choose the depth from the profile. Do not dumb the piece down just because the voice is plain.

## 5. Writing principles

1. Earn certainty: be decisive about observed evidence and calibrated about inference.
2. Speak to the reader's work: effects on decisions and workflows, not announcements.
3. Value before promotion: the insight lives inside the article.
4. Specificity over adjectives: show what changes instead of calling it "groundbreaking".
5. Explain mechanisms: "it matters because" must lead to a testable chain of reasoning.
6. Be fair to uncertainty: say what the evidence does not establish.
7. Avoid false balance: discuss competing readings in proportion to their support.

## 6. Sentences and formatting

- Active voice, plain English, technical terms only where the reader needs them.
- Vary paragraph length. A long-form article uses normal paragraphs, not one sentence per line.
- Informative headings, bullets for real sequences or checklists, tables only when a comparison needs one.
- Concrete verbs: test, audit, validate, route, measure, recover, compare.
- Second person ("if you manage...") when it helps; do not overuse rhetorical questions.

## 7. Avoid by default

Filler openers ("in today's ever-evolving landscape"), hype words ("game-changing", "revolutionary", "unlock the power of"), "everyone is talking about", unsupported predictions framed as certainty, a "not X, but Y" contrast in every section, long em-dash chains, decorative emoji, forced suspense, and generic closers ("stay ahead of the curve"). Add everything in `phrasesToAvoid`.

## 8. Contrast examples (illustrative, not facts)

- Vague: "AI automation is changing lead generation forever."
  Better: "The interesting question is not whether the tool can send a reply. It is what happens when a reply fails, an appointment moves, or a lead needs a human decision."
- Overconfident: "Every team should implement this immediately."
  Better: "For teams with this setup, the first step is to check eligibility and run a controlled test. Other teams may not be affected yet."

## 9. Funnel intent (only when the request states it)

Discovery builds relevance, depth explains decisions and tradeoffs, authority shows judgment through careful synthesis, and commercial discusses an offer only when the request explicitly calls for it. Do not mistake a high-view topic for a strong fit with the reader.
